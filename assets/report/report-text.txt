Abstract
This study compares the deflection and ideal axial buckling loads of three equal-mass beams.
A solid rectangle, a hollow box and a parabolically tapered rectangle are modeled using Euler–
Bernoulli beam elements. Under the dimensions chosen here, the hollow section is stiffest, while
the tapered beam outperforms the baseline solid beam.
These results describe an idealized
comparison, not an optimized bridge design.
1
Background and scope
The stiffness of a beam depends on the distribution of material about the neutral axis. A hollow
section can achieve a larger second moment of area when its external envelope is allowed to grow,
while tapering can place material where the transverse bending moment is greatest. These design
choices are constrained by loading, fabrication and local stability. Nishiyama and Sato studied
nonlinear bending of tapered hollow cylindrical cantilevers, a different loading and geometry from
the present linear model [1]. Sepahpour and Chang compare variable-section and prismatic beams
in an educational experimental context [2]. These studies motivate the comparison but do not
validate the present numerical predictions.
2
Model and method
Each steel beam has length L = 1 m, modulus E = 200 GPa, density 7850 kg/m3 and mass 6.28
kg. The solid baseline is 20 mm wide and 40 mm deep. The hollow section has a 40 mm by 60
mm outer envelope and a uniform wall thickness selected to match the baseline area. The tapered
section has constant width and depth h(x) = h0[1 −0.5(2x/L −1)2], with h0 selected to match the
mass.
Pinned supports are used at both ends. A transverse load of 1000 N acts at midspan. For
variable stiffness the beam equation is (EI(x)y′′)′′ = q. Cubic Hermite elements discretize the
bending energy, with two-point quadrature. A separate linear elastic eigenvalue analysis estimates
the first axial buckling load. Deflection and axial buckling are different load cases.

6
Discussion
The larger hollow envelope places material farther from the neutral axis and provides the greatest
bending stiffness among these particular sections.
Its advantage depends on the allowed outer
dimensions. The tapered section places more material near midspan, where bending moment is
highest. Neither result establishes a global optimum. The load-to-weight ratio uses the ideal axial
buckling load and is not a general measure of structural strength. Local wall buckling, yielding,
shear deformation, connections, imperfections and fabrication are outside this model. Physical tests
and additional design constraints would be needed for a practical bridge comparison.
7
Interpretation of the load cases
The midpoint transverse load and the axial compression eigenproblem answer different questions.
For the uniform baseline, symmetry and moment equilibrium give a maximum moment PL/4
at midspan, and integration of the constant-rigidity curvature gives PL3/(48EI) for the center
deflection.
The ideal axial critical load π2EI/L2 is a bifurcation load for a perfectly straight
pinned member under uniform compression.
It does not represent failure under the transverse
load and does not include yielding or local buckling. Dividing this ideal load by weight produces
a dimensionless comparison for this specific axial case; it should not be read as a general bridge
strength rating.
For a varying depth, the moment–curvature relation must retain the position dependence inside
the derivatives: (EIy′′)′′ = q. Replacing it with EIy′′′′ = q drops terms involving derivatives of
I(x). The weak formulation used here avoids that simplification and applies zero displacement

Design constraints and further work
The comparison fixes mass but permits different envelopes. The larger hollow box occupies more
space than the baseline; imposing common width or depth limits would change the feasible design
space and may change the ordering. In particular, a cavity cannot be removed from a solid rectangle
while maintaining its area within the identical envelope. A meaningful optimization would state
bounds on dimensions, minimum wall thickness, permitted taper, fabrication constraints and load
combinations, then report both objective and feasibility. The present results assess three prescribed
examples rather than solve that optimization.
A more complete study should consider local plate buckling of the box walls, stress concentra-
tions and connections, lateral stability, material yield, shear deformation, imperfections and mul-
tiple load cases. Experimental work could measure the mass, dimensions, deflections and strains
of specimens with the stated support and load conditions; numerical predictions should then be
compared with those measurements and their uncertainty. The earlier manuscript and its original
assumptions are retained in the project archive so that changes in equations, geometry, and results
can be traced.
9
Conclusion
This comparison shows how the selected cross-sectional geometries affect stiffness and ideal elastic
stability at equal mass. The hollow section benefits from its larger envelope, while the tapered
section improves the baseline response under the specified cases. Further work should examine
constrained geometry, local instability, yielding and experimental measurements before these results
are used in design.
